Chapter 2 — Lecture one: equilibrium, and why it is efficient
Start with the picture the whole course rests on. Market demand is just every buyer's unit value, sorted from high to low. Market supply is every seller's unit cost, sorted from low to high. Equilibrium is the price where excess demand and excess supply both vanish.
Oprea's line is worth memorizing: in competitive markets you don't control the price — economic fundamentals and the logic of equilibrium do. And the outcome is efficient: the market creates as much value as it can.
Efficient in three specific ways, and the exam tests the three separately. Right quantity: every unit worth more than it costs actually trades, and the price is the gatekeeper. Right producers: the cheapest units get made — if an eleven-dollar producer makes a unit that a two-dollar producer could have made, nine dollars of value is burned. Right consumers: units go to the people who value them most — and queues, lotteries, and connections are exactly what destroy that third one.
That third point is where the price-ceiling question lives, so have this sentence ready. Fewer rooms are offered than at equilibrium; trades worth more than they cost never happen; and the rooms that do get rented go to whoever books first or has connections, not to the visitors who value them most. Notice what you just did — you named wrong quantity and wrong consumers, the two of the three that a ceiling breaks. That is a full-credit answer.
The trap here is what Oprea calls fighting the equilibrium price. Price below equilibrium and you get queues, scalpers, and lost profits; above it, you don't sell. Concert tickets and sneaker drops priced "to be fair to fans" just hand the difference to bots and resellers. StubHub is the equilibrium revealed.

Chapter 3 — Lecture two: economic cost
One formula: economic cost equals expenditure, minus sunk cost, plus opportunity cost.
Opportunity cost is the value of the most highly valued alternative foregone. Sunk means already spent and unchangeable, so it is irrelevant to any forward-looking decision. Those two definitions, stated in those words, are worth points on their own.
Five applications, and the exam picks one or two. First, option value: an irreversible choice makes flexibility worth paying for. Closing a store is a one-way door, so keep it open while the loss is smaller than the option value. Second, inventory: the cost of a unit you pull from inventory is its replacement cost if you restock, and its resale value if you don't — never what you originally paid. Third, Baumol's cost disease: productivity rising elsewhere raises labour's opportunity cost, so labour-heavy sectors get more expensive. Fourth, and write this one down because it is a one-liner they like: cost of capital is the opportunity cost of money. Fifth, transfer pricing — price internal transfers at opportunity cost. If the plant is at full capacity, giving a unit to the internal division sacrifices an external sale, so charge the external price. If there is slack capacity, charge marginal cost.
The traps: the sunk cost fallacy, throwing good money after bad — Kodak, or Volvo's electric vehicle program. Missing opportunity cost, treating an asset you already own as free — the Tokyo farm, the brewery's three hundred thousand dollars of forgone lease. Pricing inventory at what you paid. And confusing accounting profit with economic profit: a firm can report a profit and still be destroying value, because the investors could have earned that return elsewhere.
One nuance that shows up as a discussion question: sticking with the old business is not always irrational. Counter positioning — Helmer's idea — says that copying the entrant would cannibalize the incumbent's existing business, and legacy divisions have their own incentives. The fix is to think like an outsider: if you were arriving today, with no history, what would you do?

Chapter 4 — Lecture three: cost curves and the price taker
Two separate decisions, and the exam rewards you for splitting them. First: open or shut? Can revenue cover the cost you can still avoid? Second: if open, how much? Set price equal to marginal cost.
Oprea's recap: a price taker maximizes profit by producing until marginal cost rises to price — and shuts down if doing that loses her money. Follow that rule at every price and you have traced out the firm's supply curve.
The relationships you will be asked to read off a graph. Marginal cost cuts average cost at average cost's minimum. Profit is the rectangle: price minus average cost at q star, times q star. So profit is positive whenever price is above average cost — which is the range between the two quantities where the average cost curve crosses that price. And because marginal cost cuts average cost at its minimum, the lowest price at which the firm can make a positive profit is the minimum of average cost.
Now the fixed cost question. A fixed cost enters neither marginal revenue nor marginal cost, so the quantity does not change. Only average cost shifts up. The firm exits only if price falls below the new minimum average cost.
Shutdown, precisely: if the fixed cost is avoidable, you need price at or above minimum average cost. If the fixed cost is sunk, keep producing at price equals marginal cost even at a loss, as long as price covers variable cost — the loss is smaller than the fixed cost you would eat anyway. The bakery example had average costs of forty-six, twenty-seven, twenty-one seventy, twenty, and twenty, all above a twelve dollar price, so it stays closed. The exceptions are real too: airlines flew empty "ghost flights" because losing the airport slot was the avoidable cost of not flying, and plants run at small losses because restarting burns fuel and time.
Two fallacies to name out loud. The average-cost fallacy: turning away an order, or shutting down, when price is below average cost but above avoidable cost. And the shared-overhead fallacy: allocating overhead per unit and killing a product line that actually covers its marginal cost — John Deere, and the bacon cones. There is an incentive twist worth a sentence: divisions have every reason to push their costs into the shared pool. And cost-plus pricing is the same error dressed as a policy — it marks up an allocated "full cost" and prices you out of profitable orders.
Last piece: market supply is the horizontal sum of firms' marginal cost curves. Flatter marginal cost means a low-cost firm that supplies a lot before its costs climb; steeper means high-cost. Low-cost firms earn a surplus and the marginal firm just breaks even — ERCOT dispatches the cheapest generators first and the peakers last. In the long run, entry competes profits away unless the edge cannot be copied.

Chapter 5 — Lecture four: demand, elasticity, and comparative statics
Demand is marginal benefit, which is willingness to pay for one more unit. Market demand is the horizontal sum: at a given price you add quantities, never prices. A change in the good's own price moves you along the curve. Anything else — substitutes, complements, income, quality, expectations — shifts the curve.
Now the four fingerprints. Read a shock backwards. Price up and quantity up means demand shifted out. Price up and quantity down means supply shifted in. Price down and quantity up means supply shifted out. Price down and quantity down means demand shifted in. Memorize those four; they turn a paragraph of story into an instant answer.
When both curves move, one variable is pinned down and the other is ambiguous. Demand out and supply out: quantity rises, price ambiguous. Demand out and supply in: price rises, quantity ambiguous. Here is the sentence to have ready: both shocks push price up, but they push quantity in opposite directions, so the net change in quantity depends on which shift is larger. For example, a wage increase raises the cost of each room, shifting supply in — price up, quantity down — while a demand shifter moves demand out — price up, quantity up.
Elasticity. Epsilon equals percentage change in quantity divided by percentage change in price. Absolute value above one is elastic, below one is inelastic. It is unit-free, which is why it is comparable across goods and slopes are not.
And the incidence rule, which is the single highest-value sentence in this section: a shock lands on price when the other curve is steep — inelastic — and on quantity when the other curve is flat — elastic. Concretely: short-run demand for eggs is nearly vertical, so when supply shifts in, the new crossing is far above the old one but barely to the left. The shock lands on price.
Two corollaries. The more inelastic demand is, the more of a cost increase sellers can pass on to buyers — that was quiz four, the insulin question; the cost increase reaches the buyers almost entirely. And in the long run both curves are flatter, so high prices are self-correcting: supply is flatter, and the price spike gives most of its gain back through entry, capacity, and substitution. Which lets you answer the "is this price gouging" question the way Oprea wants: the price doubled because supply fell against inelastic demand — scarcity, not gouging.
The traps here. Treating a short-run spike as permanent — Solyndra, Amazon's COVID build-out. Reading tea leaves, treating price moves as random or as rivals' moods and morals. And treating a scatter of price-quantity points as your demand curve: both curves are moving, so market data mixes supply and demand shifts. You never see the demand curve directly; it has to be inferred from how quantity responds to price, which is why you need an A/B test or a natural experiment — Uber's rounding, ZipRecruiter's price variation.
One more that gets a full question sometimes: Jevons paradox. More efficient steam engines burned more coal, not less. Resource used equals units of service times resource per unit. The efficiency gain lowers the effective price by roughly the gain times the input's cost share, and quantity rises by elasticity times that, so you get a backfire when elasticity times share exceeds one.

Chapter 6 — Lecture five: monopoly, the price maker
The one rule: a price maker sets marginal revenue equal to marginal cost, and then reads the price off the demand curve. Price stays above marginal cost. Never, ever set price equal to marginal cost for a monopolist. That was quiz five, and it was the single most common wrong answer.
Why is marginal revenue below price? Because selling one more unit requires cutting the price on all the units you have already sold. Marginal revenue equals price plus the slope times quantity — the gain on the new unit minus the price cut on everything before it.
The linear recipe, step by step, spoken out. Step one: if you are given quantity as a function of price, invert it. Q equals c minus d P becomes P equals c over d minus q over d. Step two: with demand P equals a minus b q, marginal revenue is a minus two b q — same intercept, twice as steep. Step three: set a minus two b q equal to marginal cost, so q star equals a minus marginal cost, over two b. Step four: price star equals a plus marginal cost, over two — use that as your check. Step five: profit equals price star minus marginal cost, times q star, minus fixed cost. And the competitive quantity is exactly twice q star.
Notice step five: profit is price minus marginal cost, times quantity. Not price minus average cost, when marginal cost is constant and you handle the fixed cost separately.
Areas on the graph. Consumer surplus is the triangle above the price and below demand. Deadweight loss is the triangle between demand and marginal cost, from q star out to the competitive quantity — value that is never created.
Three exam-favourite implications. First: a fixed cost, like rent, does not change marginal revenue, marginal cost, or the optimal price. It only affects whether to operate at all. Second: she should be maximizing profit, not hitting a revenue target — with lower demand, marginal revenue shifts in, so the profit-maximizing quantity and the price both fall. Third: in the inelastic region of demand, marginal revenue is negative — the extra unit doesn't offset the price cut — so you should raise the price; quantity falls but revenue rises by more.
The markup formula, if they ask: price equals marginal cost divided by one plus one over epsilon. With epsilon of negative two and marginal cost of ten, price is twenty. With epsilon of negative four, price is thirteen thirty-three. A price taker faces infinitely elastic demand, so price equals marginal cost. And the Lerner index — price minus marginal cost, over price — equals negative one over epsilon, running from zero to one, with perfect competition scoring zero.
And the framing point that shows up in the discussion questions: only demand and cost limit a monopolist's profit. A monopolist still fights costs, and still cares what rivals do to demand. Having a competitor doesn't switch you from price maker to price taker — it shifts and flattens your demand curve. A new competitor makes your demand more elastic, so your optimal markup falls, but you are still setting marginal revenue equal to marginal cost.

Chapter 7 — Lecture six: two-part tariff and price discrimination
The force: a single monopoly price leaves value uncreated — that's the deadweight loss — and uncaptured — that's consumer surplus. The two-part tariff fixes both.
The optimal two-part tariff: set the per-unit price equal to marginal cost, and set the fixed fee equal to the entire consumer surplus at that price. The unit price buys the efficient quantity; the fee captures the surplus. Disney with zero marginal cost makes the rides free and charges two hundred dollars at the gate. The County Fair example: demand P equals forty minus two q, marginal cost of eight, so the per-unit price is eight, quantity is sixteen, and the fee is one half times sixteen times thirty-two, equals two hundred fifty-six. Total is three hundred eighty-four, against one hundred twenty-eight from the best single price.
Comparative statics on the fee, because they love these. Marginal cost goes up, so the unit price goes up, so quantity falls, so the triangle shrinks and the fee falls. A parallel downward shift in demand also shrinks the triangle, so the fee falls. If marginal cost slopes up, the rule is unchanged — set the unit price where marginal cost equals demand — but there can be some producer surplus left at that price.
The limits of the two-part tariff: arbitrage — Costco's dark side, people reselling; uncertainty — overshoot the fee and you lose the whole market; and heterogeneity — one fee cannot fit different customer types, which is what pushes you to price discrimination.
Price discrimination is adding a different markup over cost to different buyers. Price differences that merely reflect cost differences are not price discrimination. A senior movie ticket is pure discrimination. Overnight versus ground shipping is a mix. A business-class seat is mostly a cost difference.
Third degree is segment pricing — one price within a group, different prices across groups, like round-trip airfares. First degree is individualized — groups of one, with perfect insight into willingness to pay. Second degree, menus and versioning, is lecture seven and not examinable.
Three conditions, and here is the distinction to be precise about. You must be able to identify types — that is, know which group a buyer belongs to. And separately, you must be able to separate them — that is, actually make each group pay its own price. Identifying is knowing; separating is enforcing, with a fence: an ID check, an advance purchase requirement, a Saturday-night stay. Knowing without a fence means everyone simply claims the low price. The third condition is no arbitrage: low-price buyers must not be able to resell to high-price ones.
Each condition breaks in its own way. Identification breaks when you have no data that predicts willingness to pay. Separation breaks on legal limits — race, religion, gender — on customer backlash, and on buyers disguising themselves; product segmentation is how firms get around it, which is what the "pink tax" is. Arbitrage breaks it when resale is easy.
Run the ClipperJet numbers once, out loud. Marginal cost is two. Business demand: P equals ten minus Q, so marginal revenue is ten minus two Q, set equal to two, Q equals four, price is six, profit is six minus two times four, sixteen dollars. Leisure: P equals six minus Q, marginal revenue six minus two Q equals two, Q equals two, price four, profit four minus two times two, four dollars. Separating gives twenty dollars. The best single price gives eighteen. So the value of discriminating is two dollars — and the further apart the two ideal prices are, the bigger that gain.
And the closing line: the optimal price depends on the customer's level of willingness to pay and their price responsiveness. Profitable firms leverage differences across segments to charge better targeted prices.

Chapter 9 — Ten sentences to have on the tip of your tongue
Listen to these once, then say them back.
In competitive markets you don't control the price; fundamentals and equilibrium do.
The money already spent is sunk and is not an economic cost; owning it does not make it free.
The relevant cost of a unit from inventory is its replacement cost if you restock, or its resale value if you don't.
A price taker produces where price equals marginal cost, so the market price must be that number.
A fixed cost enters neither marginal revenue nor marginal cost, so quantity does not change; only average cost shifts up.
Shocks land on price when the other curve is steep, and on quantity when it is flat.
High prices are self-correcting: in the long run supply is flatter, so the spike gives most of its gain back.
A price maker sets marginal revenue equal to marginal cost, then reads the price off the demand curve.
Deadweight loss is the triangle between demand and marginal cost, from the monopoly quantity out to the competitive quantity — value never created.
Set the per-unit price equal to marginal cost so customers buy the efficient quantity, then set the fee equal to the consumer surplus at that price.
